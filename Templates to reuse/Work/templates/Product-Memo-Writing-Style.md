# Product Memo Writing Style

Derived from Jason's memos: *The 3 Layers of Quality for Ito* and *Zero Config Positioning Challenges*.

## Core Principle

Every document makes one argument. State it up front. Everything else is evidence, structure, or implication. Readers should never wonder what the document is trying to prove.

---

## Structure

**Argument-first.** Open with the thesis, not context or background. The first paragraph tells the reader what they will believe by the end.

**Evidence + interpretation as a unit.** Quotes, data, and customer examples are never left to speak for themselves. Each is followed immediately by what it means. The interpretation is the value; the evidence is proof.

**Headers advance the argument, not just the topic.** Headers should read as claims: "Quality is our most important factor right now." Not "Quality." If a header could be swapped with any other section's header without changing meaning, rewrite it.

**Layered specificity.** Open broad, drill into evidence, return to the implication. A reader can stop at any layer and still have a complete thought.

**Pattern → Evidence → Signal.** When presenting multiple examples (e.g., customer calls), name the pattern first, show the cases, then state explicitly what the pattern means.

---

## Voice and Tone

**Unhedged assertions.** Write "Quality matters most." Not "Quality may be the most important factor." Not "It seems like quality could be prioritized." If you believe it, say it directly.

**Honest about tradeoffs.** Acknowledge the strongest version of the opposing view before explaining why the argument still holds. This is not hedging; it is earning credibility before landing the conclusion.

**No filler.** No transitional throat-clearing ("It is worth noting that..."). No padding ("As we can see from the above..."). Cut it.

**Short sentences to land the point.** After a longer build, one short declarative sentence delivers the conclusion. "That is the root of our quality problem."

---

## Formatting

**Prose carries the argument.** Bullet points are for constraints, options, or lists of evidence. They do not replace analytical sentences. If you are using bullets to avoid writing a paragraph, write the paragraph.

**Bold for key concepts and conclusions**, not for decoration. When a reader skims bold text, they should get the argument spine.

**Italics for emphasis within quotes** or for a phrase that needs distinction. Use sparingly.

**Tables only for dense comparative data** where rows have parallel structure (e.g., customer-by-feature grid). Not for simple lists.

**Questions at the end are tactical tools.** If a memo ends with questions, they are specific, purposeful, and framed for action (discovery calls, team discussion, research). Not rhetorical musing.

---

## What to Avoid

- Passive voice as the default
- Academic hedging ("it could be argued," "one might consider")
- Bullets as a substitute for reasoning
- Headers that describe rather than argue ("Background," "Context," "Overview")
- Summarizing what you just wrote at the end unless the document is long enough to need a navigation aid
- Em dashes (never)
- Vague qualifiers ("somewhat," "fairly," "rather")

---

## Audience Assumption

Write for a sharp, time-constrained reader at the leadership or senior IC level. They do not need concepts explained. They need argument, evidence, and implication delivered efficiently. If something is obvious to that reader, cut it.

## Know Your Reader: Team Dossiers

When writing a memo for a specific person or leadership audience, consult the team dossiers before drafting:

**`PM/people/team-dossiers.md`** — persuasion and communication profiles for Evan, Barron, Mike, and Grayson, built from ~60 recorded meetings.

Quick reference:

- **Evan** (CTO): data first, then argument. Frame through customer trust. Show your reasoning, not just your conclusion. Acknowledge tradeoffs before he finds them himself.
- **Barron** (CEO): lead with competitive urgency and business metrics. Anchor to the fundraising story. Give him a decisive recommendation, not a menu of options. Show the trajectory, not just the current state.
- **Mike** (Engineering Director): open with the specific friction being eliminated. Use a real engineer's workflow, not an idealized one. Lead with the simplest version; restraint earns trust.
- **Grayson** (Head of Sales): connect to a specific customer win or objection. Show what changes in his next sales call. Give binary answers where possible; he needs to move fast.

When multiple readers are likely (e.g., a memo going to both Evan and Barron), identify where their instincts conflict and resolve it in the framing rather than writing two different memos.
