# Copywriting Reference

Sources: Jason's personal notes on copywriting (primary, marked **[Notes]**) and John Caples / Fred Hahn's *Tested Advertising Methods* (fills gaps and adds detail, marked **[Book]**). Where the two overlap, the notes' framing is used and the book adds supporting detail or extra items. Chapter references below (e.g. "Ch5") refer to *Tested Advertising Methods*.

## 1. Philosophy

- **Test everything. Measure. Never trust opinion over a real result.** [Notes/Book] Expert judges (even good copywriters) pick the losing ad roughly half the time when compared against actual sales results. Confidence in a headline is not a substitute for testing it. (Ch3, Ch17, Ch18)
- **What you say matters more than how you say it.** [Notes] Beautifully written copy with the wrong appeal still fails. Style without substance is worthless. (Ch6, Ch10)
- **Caples's three-step approach to any piece of copy:** [Notes]
  1. **Attention** - nothing happens unless something stops the prospect long enough to engage.
  2. **Interest** - keep it focused on the prospect: what do THEY get.
  3. **Action** - if it doesn't convert enough prospects, it failed, no matter how clever or well-written.
- **"Philosophy of As If":** [Book] Write every piece, even brand/institutional copy, as if it must sell or die like a mail-order ad: strong headline, real art, real copy, a real reason to act now. Don't imitate untested "award show" ads; imitate ads that get run again and again (repetition over time is itself evidence they're winning). (Ch7)

## 2. Headlines

The headline is the most important element in most ads, roughly 50-75% of an ad's effectiveness. Assume the headline is the only way you'll get anyone to read the rest of the copy; scores of people read only headlines for every one who reads both headline and body. [Notes/Book] (Ch2)

### Three classes of successful headlines [Notes/Book] (Ch2, Ch3)
1. **Self-interest** (by far the most important). Make the headline about a benefit to the reader, something they get from you.
2. **News.** New features, discoveries, announcements.
3. **Curiosity** (weakest alone). "For every curiosity headline that succeeds, a dozen will fail." Curiosity works well when it's paired with an implied benefit; on its own it's a coin flip at best. (Ch17)

### Rules and pointers [Notes/Book] (Ch2, Ch3, Ch4, Ch14, Ch15)
- Be specific. Speak to your target audience about something they want badly. Specific numbers beat vague ones ("97,482" beats "nearly 100,000"; a precise "$27... to $75,000" beats "this pays financially").
- Be positive. Avoid the gloomy angle when possible.
- Suggest how quick and easy something is, but keep it believable. Too-good-to-be-true claims ("work less and earn more") fail.
- Be believable. Don't sound like a scam.
- If a headline is long, bold or otherwise call out only the words that carry real independent meaning (not connector words), and put that meaningful phrase at the start for maximum impact. Cap at most six or seven words in all-caps; longer runs get hard to read.
- The best ads put the key appeal directly in the headline, not just in the body copy. In a real case study, every high-performing ad in a set had its core appeal in the headline; none of the underperformers did.
- Don't sacrifice clarity for brevity. Say the complete thought even if it takes 20+ words. Long headlines are fine if they say something real, better a longer headline that says something than a short one that says nothing.
- Avoid the "dead"/pompous headline (sounds like a bronze plaque), the "too smart" headline that just makes people say "how clever," the meaningless headline, and the "hard to grasp" headline that needs a second read. Readers give you one involuntary instant, then move on.
- Write the headline with the brand/logo in mind, its meaning can change depending on whose name is attached ("End Money Worries" means something different for a bank vs. an insurer).
- Headline and any accompanying image must tell the SAME story. Readers react to the picture before the words; an ironic or clever mismatch between headline and image reliably gets misread literally.
- Don't run copy without a headline (exception: a single stunning, self-explanatory product photo).
- Avoid contractions and letter combos that visually distort at a glance ("We're" misread as "Were"; "Here's how!" reads like "Howl"). Read it aloud, or have someone else read it aloud, if they stumble, rewrite that spot.
- Avoid words that need explaining or have been diluted through overuse ("exclusive," "guaranteed"). Replace them or spell out the concrete substance behind the claim.
- Write at roughly a sixth-grade reading level. Don't assume a word is universally known; test on people of average, general background before running anything "clever."

### Writing process [Notes/Book] (Ch4, Ch14)
- Write MANY headlines (aim for 25+) before picking one. Set them aside a day, then choose from the "bored customer" point of view, don't trust your own judgment alone, you're too close to it.
- Show a shortlist to someone who hasn't seen it before. Never show just one option and ask "is this good?" (people are too polite to say no). Show two and ask which is better and why, that reliably produces honest critique of both.
- Never settle for a first draft. Set it aside overnight, then try to improve it by shortening, lengthening, or fully reconstructing it.
- Small, testable word swaps can move response substantially: adding a selecting word ("Car owners!") lifted response 20%; adding a benefit word lifted 27%; adding an age-qualifier increased both volume and lead quality; a simpler synonym lifted orders 20%. Always test rather than assume.

### Proven opening formulas [Notes/Book, merged] (Ch5)
Use as a springboard for angles, not a crutch:

Introducing / Announcing / an announcement-quality word (Finally, Presenting, Just Published, Good-bye old way) / New / Now / At Last / a specific date / news style / feature the price / feature a reduced price / a special merchandising offer / an easy-payment plan / a free offer (visually emphasized, doesn't need to be the first word) / offer information of value / tell a story / How To / How / Why / Which / Who Else / Wanted / This / Because / If / Advice (low-resistance, reads as free counsel not a pitch) / a testimonial (real or in testimonial voice) / offer the reader a test ("Can your scalp pass the fingernail test?") / a one-, two-, or three-word headline (only if genuinely meaningful) / warn the reader to delay buying (pattern interrupt) / let the advertiser speak directly, first person / address a specific person or group ("To car owners who...") / ask a question / lead with facts and figures.

## 3. Finding the Right Appeal

- Base appeals: sex/love/affection, greed (what money can buy), fear (of loss or of missing out), duty/honor/professionalism. [Book] (Ch6)
- Broader proven appeal list: make more money, save money, retirement/old-age security, better health now, health-care security, advance in profession, prestige, enjoyment, easier chores, more leisure, comfort, reduce weight, freedom from worry, belong to the "in" group, get a bargain, be popular/attract attention, outshine the neighbors (often best implied, not stated). [Book] (Ch6)
- All appeals should focus on what's best for the BUYER, never what's best for the seller. Avoid "world's best seed"; lead with "world's best lawn." [Book]
- Old-style "you can make big money easily" claims read as scammy today. The modern version must be more specific, believable, and honest (e.g. "plan" instead of a guaranteed outcome). [Book]
- Specific appeal beats general/institutional appeal. "Better automobile values" (vague) flopped; naming the actual make/year/price tripled response. [Book]
- Four-step process: (1) have an actual reason for the appeals you're testing, not a hunch, (2) test several different appeals, (3) determine the winner from real results, (4) then use that winning appeal everywhere, not just where you tested it. [Book]
- "Be your own guinea pig" is a fine starting signal (if you react strongly to one appeal over another, that's informative) but always confirm with a real test, audience specifics change the answer. [Book]

## 4. First Paragraph / Opening

Three rules: [Notes/Book] (Ch9)
1. **Keep it short.** A long opening paragraph is less likely to be read.
2. **Build on the headline.** Continue its exact thought; switching ideas between headline and body loses the reader who was hooked on the original promise.
3. **State the most important benefit immediately.** "What do I get?" is what keeps people reading.

Six proven opening devices (from Reader's Digest-style analysis, useful for ad/web openers too): [Book] (Ch9)
1. An interrupting idea, a startling/odd statement that breaks autopilot.
2. The shocker, a jarring, surprising fact.
3. News, plainly stating a new development.
4. Preview, a compressed taste of what's coming.
5. A strong quotation that frames the piece.
6. A story or anecdote.

If stuck, write "This is a story about..." as a crutch opening, then delete that phrase, what's left is often a strong direct opening. [Book]

## 5. Body Copy

### Styles to use [Notes/Book, merged] (Ch10)
Straightforward (plain facts, no rhetoric) / story copy / "you and me" copy (direct, chatty, first person) / imaginative copy (vivid but still benefit-centered) / factual copy ("the more you tell, the more you sell") / facts-plus-style (stylish voice AND packed with real facts) / forthright copy (admit real weaknesses, builds believability) / superlative copy (bold self-praise, but only backed by real facts) / signed copy (a named person signs it, builds trust) / title copy (short, punchy names beat long descriptive ones) / teaser copy (a challenge-style opening that seems to discourage the reader, works because it's unusual and self-selecting) / news-page style (formatted like editorial content, must be clearly labeled advertising, use sparingly) / competitive copy (direct comparison with real numbers).

Use with caution: card copy (logo-and-slogan only, rarely drives enough volume), clever copy (usually either fails to be clever or fails to sell, or both; rare hybrids succeed but the odds are stacked against you, default to straightforward selling instead), humorous copy (narrows your audience to whoever shares that sense of humor).

Avoid entirely: poetic copy (showcases the writer, not the product), affected/purple prose, and unbelievable/exaggerated copy (erodes trust permanently, a betrayed prospect today is a skeptical customer for life).

### Twenty ways to increase selling power [Book, condensed] (Ch11)
1. Present tense, second person. "You feel..." not "A man will feel..." Keep hammering you, you, you.
2. Use subheads: they tell the story in brief to skimmers and re-hook readers who start to drift.
3. Caption every illustration; captions get read even more than body copy and should carry the brand name and/or promise.
4. Simple style: short words, short sentences, plain structure.
5. Prefer simple words over pompous/Latinate ones, less mental effort to decode means more attention reaches the actual idea.
6. Sequence free info before sales talk. If the pitch comes first, many readers never reach the part that earns their trust.
7. Prefer selling copy (claims backed by specific proof) over style copy (unsupported flowery adjectives). When in doubt, choose proof over adjectives.
8. Arouse curiosity but don't fully satisfy it in the copy itself, make the reader take the next step (read on, click through) to get the payoff.
9. Make copy specific. Precise numbers read as fact; round numbers read as a claim.
10. Long copy (500-1,500 words) consistently outpulls short copy wherever you can trace results, mail order, book clubs, correspondence courses, real estate, classifieds, because it lets you include every relevant argument for the fraction of readers who are real prospects. Exception: where a single image truly tells the whole story.
11. Write MORE copy than the space needs, then cut it down. Cutting improves copy, the smallest version is often the best-written version. Copy is like broth: the more you reduce it, the stronger the flavor.
12. Sell your specific product, not the generic category, or you're just helping every competitor equally.
13. Apply the same headline/opening/subhead rules to direct mail and email-to-prospects; in a letter, the first sentence functions as the headline.
14. Understatement often out-pulls old-school hype today. Conservative, factual, confident tone can out-convert breathless "amazing/incredible" claims, default toward understatement and credibility.
15. Avoid trick slogans or wordplay that's provably untrue. A plainer, true claim beats a clever, false one.
16. Get real feedback the right way: never show one option and ask "is this good?" Show two and ask which is better and why.
17. If a sales call follows, don't say "a salesperson will call" in the copy, it can cut response by 75%+.
18. Study how mail-order catalogs (Sears, L.L. Bean) sell products in pure sales copy, they're masters of it.
19. Make every single page/ad a complete, standalone sales pitch. Never assume the reader saw a previous page or will see the next one.
20. Always end with a clear call to action, ideally with a real, specific reason for urgency rather than urgency for its own sake.

### Enthusiasm and drafting process [Book] (Ch8)
- Enthusiasm is as persuasive in copy as in personal selling. A genuinely excited, believing voice outsells technique alone.
- If the start feels cold: just write throwaway copy to warm up (expect to discard it), or start in the middle and mine your own draft for the real opening, or write fast and edit later (editing is much easier than generating from nothing).
- Capture enthusiasm the day you feel it, it cools and dilutes if you wait.
- Mentally banish the internal critic (manager, client) while writing the first hot draft. Write it uncensored, THEN go back in cold blood and cut what a critic would object to. Writing for imagined critics from the start produces "correct but lifeless" copy.
- "Reason why" copy (appeals to intelligence, gets agreement) and emotional copy (drives actual action) work together. Skillfully combined, they get people out of their chairs.
- You can tame wild, enthusiastic, rough copy into something great through editing. You cannot resuscitate copy that was dead (passionless) from the start. **Write hot, edit cold.**

## 6. Web-Specific Scannability [Notes]

The book predates the web; this section is the load-bearing addition for website copy specifically.

- 79% of people on the web don't read, they scan. There should always be at least one scannable element visible on the page at any given time.
- We're bad at processing vague ideas, abstract concepts, unordered relationships. Start gentle and raise the action gradually, a screaming headline or hyped lede pulls people in but won't keep them to the end.
- For clarity, explain both the benefit AND the feature, then their relationship. Use bullets with the benefit bolded, feature explained after. Example pattern: "**[Benefit, bolded]**, thanks to/because of [feature explanation]."
- Meaningful subheads that summarize major points or tease the reader forward (mini-headlines).
- Bullet lists that itemize features or benefits.
- Boldface to highlight keywords and important benefits.
- Short paragraphs, one idea each (a scanner reading only the first few words of a paragraph will miss a second idea buried later).
- Images that convey value more forcibly than copy could: charts, graphs, high-quality product photos.
- Captions get read by 50% more prospects than body copy and have close to 100% recall, always caption images with the selling point, not just a literal description.

## 7. Offers & Calls to Action

Merged checklist (notes + book), grouped by purpose: [Notes/Book] (Ch13)

**Make the offer itself land:**
- Mention the offer in the headline, not just the body, and again in a subhead.
- Emphasize "Free" visually, but it must be unconditional or all conditions disclosed immediately next to it.
- Show a picture of what they're getting (booklet, sample), even shrunk small; keep any title text on it legible.
- Give the lead magnet/offer a genuinely good, benefit-driven title.
- Write an attractive, specific description of the offer, list real contents (page count, chapters, a table-of-contents preview works well).
- Mention the offer again at the end (twice total, minimum).
- Get a credible, relevant person to write the foreword/endorsement.
- Include testimonials, ideally several representing different reader segments.
- Sweeten the offer (a bonus, a second freebie) if the base offer isn't pulling.

**Make it easy and low-risk to respond:**
- Include a coupon/response mechanism with a stated dollar value; put real selling copy inside the coupon itself, not a blank form. (Web equivalent: a form/CTA block that restates the value, not just a blank field.)
- Print contact info more than once in case part of the piece is lost or scrolled past.
- Include a phone number, ideally toll-free, and flag if it works for local calls too (people assume otherwise).
- Emphasize "no obligation" explicitly.
- For sensitive topics, offer a plain, unbranded option (privacy-preserving equivalents apply online too).
- Urge immediate action with a real incentive or deadline, not manufactured urgency.
- If a sales call follows sign-up, don't say so in the copy (cuts response 75%+).

**Test and track:**
- Test multiple offers against each other before scaling the winner.
- Test multiple executions of the same winning offer.
- Test which channel is actually most efficient, don't assume.
- Test ad/page size or format; bigger isn't always better.
- Study competitors' offers, especially ones they keep running (repetition is evidence of a winner).
- Keep rigorous, itemized records of cost-per-response so you can compare and reallocate.
- Watch seasonality and timing for your specific product and customers.

## 8. Images & Layout

Proven picture types, in order of general reliability: [Notes/Book] (Ch15)
1. The product itself.
2. The product in use.
3. The reward of using the product.
4. The end result / underlying ambition attained (a diploma, a promotion).
5. A zoomed-in/enlarged detail.
6. Something genuinely dramatic, but relevant to the product's promise.

Rules: [Notes/Book]
- Prefer photographs over illustrations/paintings, photos read as proof, drawings read as an idealized fantasy.
- Faces, especially looking directly at the viewer, are excellent attention-getters and crop well at small sizes (unlike landscapes, which lose meaning when shrunk).
- Every image needs a caption that carries the selling point (see Section 6).
- Attention-getting picture subjects (brides, babies, animals, celebrities, drama) only convert if the picture is actually related to the product. An irrelevant "stopper" image attracts the wrong audience in the wrong mindset.
- Put salesmanship before "fine art" in layout decisions. Test layouts on average people, not designers, to see what actually stops them.
- All-type (no image) treatments can outperform everything else when the words alone carry a complete, compelling story. Always ask: does this image add enough sales value to justify its cost in space/attention?

## 9. Analyzing and Proofing Your Work

Checklist for comparing drafts/variants side by side: [Notes/Book] (Ch6)
- **Headline/subheads:** appeal, wording, size, placement.
- **Images:** subject, size, style, placement, caption quality.
- **Layout/colors:** don't be afraid to test.
- **Copy:** amount, type, style.
- **Offer:** including how to order/sign up.
- **Size/placement:** how much space/prominence does it get relative to everything else on the page.
- **Medium:** where does it run, what kind, how long, when.

**The two-version trick:** never show a single draft and ask "is this good?" Show two versions and ask which they prefer and why they don't like the other, this reliably produces honest, useful feedback. [Notes/Book] (Ch18)

## 10. Testing, Realistically Applied

The book's full 17-way testing framework (split-runs, coupon tracking, matched-market sales tests) assumes real volume and paid media most startups don't have. The durable, stage-agnostic lessons:

- The core rule that survives at any stage: **decide what you believe and why, then look for a way, however informal, to check it against a real outcome rather than trusting your own opinion.**
- At low volume, prefer the two-version opinion test (Section 9) and qualitative signal (what do people ask, where do they hesitate, what do they misunderstand) over formal statistical testing you don't have the traffic to run.
- Continuous, cheap tracking (which page/ad/CTA got which response) still applies at any scale: key or tag your variants so you at least know which one produced results, even informally.
- Real A/B testing (Section 18 in the book) becomes worthwhile once you have enough traffic/volume for statistical significance, not before. Don't manufacture false confidence from an underpowered test.

## 11. Reality Check for Early-Stage / Startup Copy

Notes from Lars (context: applying this to an early startup's website copy): [Notes]

- Good copywriters don't actually know in advance what will work. Two reasons: (1) real wins come only after deeply understanding how your specific target market views the world, principles (features vs. benefits, problem-then-solution, AIDA) just get you started; (2) finding the best copy takes relentless testing with real data, and early-stage startups don't have the volume to do this rigorously.
- At this stage: focus on covering the basics and having good copy for the first version. Don't chase "perfect" copy, you won't know what perfect looks like until you have enough traffic to genuinely A/B test.
- If copy variants are targeting different segments/use cases, the real question isn't "which copy is better" in the abstract, it's "which market is more valuable," standard custdev answers that, not copy testing.
- For product screenshots/photos: include two, one illustrating the problem, one illustrating the solution, each with its own caption.
- Directionally, minimalist home pages have outperformed long-form for at least one real case in this space; conversion often goes up when things are cut from the homepage, even though Section 5 above says long copy generally wins when you can trace results. Don't assume long-form wins on a homepage without testing it in your own context.
